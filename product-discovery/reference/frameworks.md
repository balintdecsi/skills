# Entrepreneurship frameworks — working notes

Short, practical restatements of the frameworks the skill draws on. Each section ends with
what to *produce* when applying it. Primary sources are listed in `SKILL.md`.

## Lean Startup (Eric Ries, 2011; Steve Blank)

A startup is *a temporary organisation searching for a repeatable and scalable business
model* (Blank). Existing companies execute a model; startups look for one, so a five-year
business plan is mostly fiction.

- **Build → Measure → Learn**: the smallest build that answers a question, measured on
  behaviour, turned into a decision.
- **MVP**: the version that maximises validated learning for the least effort. A hypothesis,
  not a half-baked product.
- **Validated learning**: evidence from real customer behaviour, not opinions or vanity
  metrics.
- **Pivot or persevere**: change one element of the model (segment, problem, channel, revenue
  model, platform) when evidence says so. Slack (a game studio's internal chat tool) and
  Airbnb (air mattresses at a conference) are the classic pivots / expansions.
- Common pitfalls: overbuilding, ignoring negative data, vanity metrics.

**Produce:** a business model canvas of hypotheses, the riskiest assumption, one MVP test with
a pre-set threshold.

## Customer Development — the Four Steps (Steve Blank, 2005)

1. **Customer discovery** — does the problem exist, for whom? ("Tell me about the last time…")
2. **Customer validation** — will they use / pay for the MVP? Is the model repeatable?
3. **Customer creation** — build end-user demand; scale acquisition; optimise the funnel.
4. **Company building** — move from search to execution: departments, processes.

Steps 1–2 loop until validated; only then spend on 3. Blue River Technology's founders, after
~100 interviews, found golf courses didn't care about their robotic mower, but farmers wanted
automated weeding — discovery redirected the company.

**Produce:** interview plan for step 1; validation test with a payment or usage threshold for
step 2.

## Effectuation (Saras Sarasvathy, 2001)

From studying expert serial founders: under real uncertainty they don't predict and plan
(causation), they **control** what they can and co-create the future (effectuation). Both
logics can be combined.

1. **Bird-in-Hand** — start with your means: who you are, what you know, whom you know.
2. **Affordable Loss** — commit only what you can afford to lose, not what you hope to earn.
3. **Crazy Quilt** — partner early with people who self-select into committing; their
   commitments reshape goals and reduce uncertainty.
4. **Lemonade** — use surprises as inputs rather than deviations.
5. **Pilot-in-the-Plane** — the future is made by action, not found by forecasting.

Fits early ideation, uncertain or new markets, social ventures, and bootstrapped side
businesses. Limitation: less structure for later scaling.

**Produce:** means inventory, affordable-loss budget (time + money), list of potential
committed partners.

## Disciplined Entrepreneurship (Bill Aulet, MIT, 2013)

A sequential, rigorous process; fits first-time and technical founders, B2B, hardware,
biotech and regulated markets where a failed pivot is expensive. Six themes: who is your
customer · what can you do for them · how do they acquire it · how do you make money · how do
you design and build it · how do you scale.

The 24 steps (after a Step 0 on getting started and building a team):

1. Market segmentation (6–12 candidate segments; talk to customers)
2. Select a beachhead market
3. Build an end-user profile
4. Calculate TAM for the beachhead (bottom-up + top-down; ~$20M–$100M is healthy)
5. Profile the persona (ideally a real person; purchasing criteria in priority order)
6. Full life-cycle use case (how they find, buy, use, and talk about it)
7. High-level product specification (visual, not a prototype)
8. Quantify the value proposition (as-is vs possible state, in the persona's top priority)
9. Identify the next 10 customers (if you can't find 10, revisit the beachhead)
10. Define the core (what is hard to copy)
11. Chart the competitive position (persona's top-2 priorities as axes)
12. Determine the decision-making unit (end user, champion, economic buyer, influencers, veto)
13. Map the process to acquire a paying customer (incl. sales cycle and budget source)
14. Calculate TAM for follow-on markets
15. Design a business model ("free" is not one)
16. Set the pricing framework (value-based; start high)
17. Calculate lifetime value (LTV, ~5-year horizon, discounted)
18. Map the sales process (short, medium, long term)
19. Calculate cost of customer acquisition (COCA, include failed spend)
20. Identify key assumptions
21. Test key assumptions (cheap, fast, empirical; prepayment or LOI where possible)
22. Define the minimum viable *business* product (value + payment + feedback loop)
23. Show that "the dogs will eat the dog food" (real customers pay and use it)
24. Develop a product plan (beyond the beachhead)

**Produce:** steps 1–5 and 8 before any code; steps 15–19 before setting prices; 20–23 as the
experiment backlog.

## Jobs-to-be-Done (Clayton Christensen)

Customers "hire" products to make progress in a specific circumstance ("people don't want a
drill, they want a hole" — Levitt). Design around the job, not the feature list.

1. Identify the job — the progress sought (functional, emotional, social).
2. Map the current journey — steps taken today and where they struggle.
3. Design the MVP to do that job better than the current hire.

Job story: *"When [situation], I want to [progress], so I can [outcome]."*

**Produce:** 1–3 job stories; the competing "hires" (including non-consumption) for the
competitor table.

## Entrepreneurial Mindset (Babson College; OpenStax *Entrepreneurship* §1.3)

A way of thinking and acting rather than a method: opportunity recognition, creativity,
resilience, resourcefulness, action orientation, comfort with ambiguity. Useful as a habit
check, not as a process.

**Produce:** the next 24-hour action.

## Business Model Innovation (Osterwalder)

Startups often fail on the model, not the product. The **Business Model Canvas** fits a model
on one page: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue
streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, cost structure. In lean practice each box
is a hypothesis to test.

## Growth (Sean Ellis, Andrew Chen)

Growth built into the product rather than bought with ads: viral loops (K-factor = invites per
user × conversion), referral incentives, A/B testing, SEO, retention design (Hotmail's email
signature; LinkedIn's profile-completeness bar). Only worth pursuing after early retention
shows people stay.

## Blitzscaling vs bootstrapping

Blitzscaling (Hoffman & Yeh) prioritises speed over efficiency to win winner-take-all markets
with network effects; it needs large funding and tolerates losses (Uber worked out; WeWork
collapsed). Most revenue-first products should **not** do this: match the funding strategy to
the market dynamics. See [financing.md](financing.md).

## Impact and social entrepreneurship

Ventures that pursue a social or environmental mission while staying financially sustainable:
triple bottom line (people, planet, profit), B Corp certification, impact investing,
microfinance (Grameen Bank). If impact is a goal, define an impact metric next to the revenue
metric from the start.

## Schumpeterian background

Entrepreneurs create "new combinations" (new products, methods, markets, sources of supply,
organisational forms) that displace incumbents — creative destruction. Useful framing for
"why now?" in a pitch: what change (technology, regulation, behaviour) makes the new
combination possible today?

## The AI-era caveat

These frameworks assumed founders largely control their experiments and stack. Building on
foundation models, clouds and dominant distribution platforms adds dependency: APIs and terms
can change overnight, costs can spike, model outputs are non-deterministic, and partnerships
with platforms are asymmetric. Defensibility shifts towards proprietary data, deep domain
expertise, workflow integration and distribution. Small teams can reach large scale, but must
treat platform risk as a first-class assumption to test.
